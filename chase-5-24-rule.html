The Chase 5/24 Rule: The Five-Card Limit Nobody Told You About

Five or more personal cards in 24 months and Chase turns you down for most of its cards — no matter how good your credit is. How the rule works, and how to stay under it.

By The Card Audit 3-minute read

A business owner I know applied for a card he actually wanted — the right card for his spending, the one that would have earned him real money every month. The answer came back in about ten seconds: denied. Not because of his credit score. Not because of his income. Because of a rule he had never heard of.

That rule is called 5/24, and if you do any business with Chase, it is the first rule you need to know.

Here is what it is. If you have opened five or more personal credit cards — with any bank, not just Chase — in the last 24 months, Chase will turn you down for most of its cards. It does not matter that your credit is good. It does not matter that you pay every bill on time. Five cards in two years, and the door is closed.

The rule at a glance

5personal cards opened — with any bank — is the limit
24months is the window Chase counts back over
25thmonth, day one: the oldest card falls off your count

The part that catches people is the “any bank” part. That store card you opened for a discount at checkout? That counts. The card your spouse added you to as an authorized user? That can count too. A card you opened and closed six months later? Still counts — the rule looks at when the account was opened, not whether it is still open. Chase reads your credit report, adds up the new accounts, and if the number is five or more, the answer is no.

Now the good news, and this is the part that matters for business owners: most business credit cards do not count. The reason is simple. Most business cards never show up on your personal credit report, so Chase never sees them when it adds things up. That is true for Chase's own business cards too — an Ink Business card does not add to your count. But — and this is the catch — Chase still checks your count before it approves you for one. You need to be under five to get the Ink card in the first place.

There are a couple of exceptions worth knowing. A few banks report their business cards to your personal credit — Capital One, Discover, and TD Bank are the ones that come up most. A business card from one of those does count. Everything else usually does not.

So what do you do with this? Three things.

First, count before you apply. Pull your free credit report and count the cards opened in the last 24 months. If the number is four or less, you have room. If it is five, wait — each card falls off the count on the first day of the 25th month after it was opened, so the clock is always running in your favor.

Second, if you want Chase cards, get them first. The Sapphire and Ink cards are some of the best cards a business owner can hold, and the rule only gets in your way once you have already opened cards elsewhere. Get the Chase cards while you are under the limit, then go explore other banks.

Third, slow down on the small stuff. That store card for a one-time discount is the most expensive discount you will ever get if it costs you a Sapphire or an Ink card later. Every card you open spends one of your five slots. Make each one count.

The 5/24 rule is not written down anywhere. Chase has never published it. But it is real, it is still enforced, and it has quietly killed more good card plans than any other rule in the business. The owners who get the best cards are not the ones with the best credit. They are the ones who knew the rule before they started applying.
